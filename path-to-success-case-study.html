Case Study - Front-End Web Development

Path to Success: building a site for someone else's next step

A responsive, mobile-first website for a life coaching brand - a collapsible nav, nine services across four categories, a full pricing breakdown, and a consultation call-to-action, built end to end from provided brand content and design mockups.

1
First Step - The Brief

The Brief

Path to Success is a life coaching business built around one line of copy: "Unlock your full potential with personalized life coaching." The brand team had locked in their content and design mockups which involved the body copy, the icon system, the color language, and needed that translated into a real, responsive working site.

The design system came with a clear set of requirements: semantic, validated HTML, a mobile-first build with specific breakpoints defined in the mockups, and CSS organized cleanly enough that anyone picking up the stylesheet could follow the structure without guesswork. Styles are layered up from the smallest screen to the largest.

Scope note ->this was a solo build: one long, anchor-linked scroll (Our Services, Why Choose Us, Prices, Take the first Step) rather than separate pages, matching how the brand wanted the experience to flow.

Second Step - Content

Structuring Nine Services Into Four Categories

The brand's content broke services into a clear hierarchy, and every service came with its own icon and color already defined by the brand team: tea, lavender, pink, and gold recur across the site, and that is the real source of this case study's own accent palette.

Supportive Community sessions plus themed Focused Sessions on specific topics.

Two more categories rounded ou the offering: Workshops & Seminars (Interactive Learning) and Online Courses (flexible Learning) each following the exact same card shape as the rest, so the pattern reads as one system instead of four separate layouts.

The brand's "Why Choose Us" pitch distilled down to four short claims: Personalized Approach, Proven Results, Supportive Environment, Flexible Options, which needed their own card treatment at the next breakpoint up.

3
Third Step - Craft

Grid, Tables, and Hamburger Nav

Three layout problems needed real mobile-first thinking on this build:

  • Service Cards:nine cards across four categories laid out with CSS Grid so the column count could grow cleanly at each breakpoint instead of hardcoding widths per screen size.
  • Pricing Tables:four separate tables (One-on-One, Group, Workshops, & Seminars, Online Courses), each with a Service/Cost structure and a different number of rows.
  • Collapsible Nav:the mockups called for a hamburger-style menu (Our Services, Why Choose Us, Prices, Take the First Step) that had to work cleanly at the smallest screen size and then expand into a normal inline nav once the layout had room for it.

4
Fourth Step - Friction

Challenges

Three things gave me the most trouble on this build:

  • CSS grid for the service cards:getting nine cards across four unevenly-sized categories to lay out consistently at every breakpoint took real iteration, a grid that works cleanly at one screen width can break at te next without careful column and gap logic.
  • The four pricing tables:each table has a different number for rows, and tables resist responsive treatment by nature. Keeping all four legible and visually consistent from the smallest breakpoint up through desktop, without any one of them looking out of step with the others took more deliberate width and spacing decisions than a single table would have.
  • The hamburger menu:toggling between a collapsed icon on small screens and a full inline nav at wider breakpoints was the hardest piece. Coordinating the toggle behavior with the CSS breakpoints so it didn't break or flicker at the in-between screen sizes.
5
Fifth Step - Outcome

Outcome & What It Taught Me

9Services across 4 categories
4Pricing tables
4Mobile-first breakpoints
1Collapsible nav, built from scratch

This build pushed me further into layout mechanics I hadn't fully settled into before: CSS grid, table responsiveness, and a hamburger nav all landing int he same project. Getting a nine-card grid, four differently-shaped pricing tables, and a collapsing menu to feel like one coherent, mobile-first system rather than three separate problems was the real work here.

The nav in particular changed how I think about responsive navigation going forward. It is not just a design decision, it is a coordination problem between markup, CSS breakpoints, and interaction state that has to hold together cleanly at every screen size, not just the two or three you test first.
